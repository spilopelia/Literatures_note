# Suyu et al. 2006 — Bayesian Regularised Source Inversions in Strong Lensing

## 1. Paper Summary

This paper studies pixelated source reconstruction in strong gravitational lensing using Bayesian regularization.

For a fixed lens potential, the source reconstruction problem is linear. The paper compares different quadratic regularization forms and uses Bayesian evidence to choose the regularization strength and the preferred regularization type.

The main regularization forms are:

1. zeroth-order regularization;
2. gradient regularization;
3. curvature regularization.

The key idea is that regularization strength should not be chosen only by visual inspection or by minimizing $$\chi^2$$. Instead, it can be selected by maximizing the Bayesian evidence.

---

## 2. Forward Model

For a fixed lens model, the data model is:

$$
\mathbf{d}
=
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{s}
+
\mathbf{n}.
$$

where:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $$\mathbf{d}$$ | observed image-pixel vector |
| $$\mathbf{s}$$ | unknown source-pixel vector |
| $$\mathbf{M}$$ | lensing + PSF + pixel-mapping matrix |
| $$\mathbf{n}$$ | noise vector |

For a fixed lens model, $$\mathbf{M}$$ is fixed. Therefore, the inversion is linear in the source pixels $$\mathbf{s}$$.

Compact interpretation:

$$
\text{fixed lens model}
\quad \Rightarrow \quad
\text{fixed mapping matrix } \mathbf{M}
\quad \Rightarrow \quad
\text{linear source inversion}.
$$

If the lens model is varied, the problem becomes nonlinear because:

$$
\mathbf{M}
=
\mathbf{M}(\theta_{\rm lens}).
$$

Then one must repeatedly solve source inversions for different lens models.

---

## 3. Noise Model

The simulated images are generated by:

$$
\text{source}
\rightarrow
\text{SIE lens mapping}
\rightarrow
\text{PSF convolution}
\rightarrow
\text{add noise}.
$$

The paper uses spatially uniform, uncorrelated Gaussian noise:

$$
\mathbf{n}
\sim
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2\mathbf{I}).
$$

Therefore, the image noise covariance is:

$$
\mathbf{C}
=
\sigma^2\mathbf{I}.
$$

The inverse covariance is:

$$
\mathbf{C}^{-1}
=
\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\mathbf{I}.
$$

Here, "uniform Gaussian noise" means that every image pixel has the same Gaussian variance. It does not mean a uniform distribution.

The paper notes that Poisson noise may be more realistic for some observations, but Gaussian noise keeps the source inversion linear.

---

## 4. Likelihood and Data Penalty

For Gaussian noise:

$$
P(\mathbf{d}\mid \mathbf{s},\mathbf{f})
\propto
\exp[-E_D(\mathbf{s})],
$$

where:

$$
E_D(\mathbf{s})
=
\frac{1}{2}
(\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{s})^T
\mathbf{C}^{-1}
(\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{s}).
$$

The data penalty is half of the weighted chi-square:

$$
E_D
=
\frac{1}{2}\chi^2.
$$

If:

$$
\mathbf{C}
=
\sigma^2\mathbf{I},
$$

then:

$$
E_D(\mathbf{s})
=
\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}
\|\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{s}\|_2^2.
$$

The role of $$\mathbf{C}^{-1}$$ is to weight residuals by the noise level.

If pixel $$i$$ has independent noise variance $$\sigma_i^2$$, then:

$$
\mathbf{C}^{-1}
=
\mathrm{diag}
\left(
\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2},
\frac{1}{\sigma_2^2},
\dots,
\frac{1}{\sigma_{N_d}^2}
\right).
$$

Low-noise pixels are weighted more strongly. High-noise pixels are weighted less strongly.

---

## 5. Regularized Source Inversion

The source is reconstructed by minimizing:

$$
E(\mathbf{s})
=
E_D(\mathbf{s})
+
\lambda E_R(\mathbf{s}).
$$

Here:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $$E_D$$ | data mismatch penalty |
| $$E_R$$ | regularization penalty |
| $$\lambda$$ | regularization strength |

The regularization penalty is quadratic:

$$
E_R(\mathbf{s})
=
\frac{1}{2}
\mathbf{s}^T\mathbf{H}\mathbf{s}.
$$

where $$\mathbf{H}$$ is the regularization matrix.

The most probable source is:

$$
\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP}
=
\arg\min_{\mathbf{s}}
\left[
E_D(\mathbf{s})+\lambda E_R(\mathbf{s})
\right].
$$

Since both terms are quadratic in $$\mathbf{s}$$, the solution is obtained from a linear system.

Define:

$$
\mathbf{A}
=
\mathbf{M}^T\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{M}
+
\lambda\mathbf{H}.
$$

Then:

$$
\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP}
=
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
\mathbf{M}^T\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{d}.
$$

The matrix $$\mathbf{A}$$ is the posterior precision / Hessian matrix for the source.

---

## 6. Types of Regularization

The paper compares three quadratic regularization forms.

### 6.1 Zeroth-Order Regularization

Zeroth-order regularization penalizes the source brightness itself:

$$
E_R(\mathbf{s})
=
\frac{1}{2}
\sum_i s_i^2.
$$

Interpretation:

- penalizes large source pixel values;
- suppresses noisy isolated pixels;
- can bias the source toward being too faint;
- similar to an $$L_2$$ penalty on source intensity.

### 6.2 Gradient Regularization

Gradient regularization penalizes differences between neighboring source pixels:

$$
E_R(\mathbf{s})
=
\frac{1}{2}
\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}
(s_i-s_j)^2.
$$

Interpretation:

- penalizes first derivatives;
- encourages smoothness;
- allows extended structures;
- suppresses pixel-to-pixel noise.

### 6.3 Curvature Regularization

Curvature regularization penalizes second derivatives:

$$
E_R(\mathbf{s})
=
\frac{1}{2}
\sum_i
(\nabla^2 s_i)^2.
$$

Interpretation:

- penalizes rapid changes in the gradient;
- prefers smoothly varying source profiles;
- allows broad gradients but suppresses sharp curvature and noisy wiggles.

For smooth simulated sources, the paper finds that curvature regularization can be preferred by Bayesian evidence.

---

## 7. Bayesian Evidence

The evidence is the probability of the observed data under a given lens model, regularization form, and regularization strength:

$$
P(\mathbf{d}\mid \lambda,\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}).
$$

It is obtained by marginalizing over all possible source reconstructions:

$$
P(\mathbf{d}\mid \lambda,\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g})
=
\int
P(\mathbf{d}\mid \mathbf{s},\mathbf{f})
P(\mathbf{s}\mid \lambda,\mathbf{g})
\,d\mathbf{s}.
$$

This is different from asking only how good the best-fit source is. The evidence asks how probable the data are after accounting for all allowed source solutions under a given prior.

Therefore, the evidence automatically balances:

- image fit;
- source complexity;
- prior volume;
- posterior volume.

---

## 8. Gaussian Prior from Regularization

The quadratic regularization defines a Gaussian source prior:

$$
P(\mathbf{s}\mid \lambda,\mathbf{g})
=
\frac{|\lambda\mathbf{H}|^{1/2}}
{(2\pi)^{N_s/2}}
\exp[-\lambda E_R(\mathbf{s})].
$$

Since:

$$
E_R(\mathbf{s})
=
\frac{1}{2}
\mathbf{s}^T\mathbf{H}\mathbf{s},
$$

the prior is:

$$
P(\mathbf{s}\mid \lambda,\mathbf{g})
=
\frac{|\lambda\mathbf{H}|^{1/2}}
{(2\pi)^{N_s/2}}
\exp
\left[
-\frac{\lambda}{2}
\mathbf{s}^T\mathbf{H}\mathbf{s}
\right].
$$

Thus, the prior precision matrix is:

$$
\lambda\mathbf{H}.
$$

Increasing $$\lambda$$ makes the prior narrower and enforces stronger regularization.

---

## 9. Derivation of the Log Evidence

The likelihood is:

$$
P(\mathbf{d}\mid \mathbf{s},\mathbf{f})
=
\frac{|\mathbf{C}^{-1}|^{1/2}}
{(2\pi)^{N_d/2}}
\exp[-E_D(\mathbf{s})].
$$

The prior is:

$$
P(\mathbf{s}\mid \lambda,\mathbf{g})
=
\frac{|\lambda\mathbf{H}|^{1/2}}
{(2\pi)^{N_s/2}}
\exp[-\lambda E_R(\mathbf{s})].
$$

The exponent is:

$$
E(\mathbf{s})
=
E_D(\mathbf{s})
+
\lambda E_R(\mathbf{s}).
$$

Because this is quadratic, expand around the most probable source:

$$
E(\mathbf{s})
=
E(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})
+
\frac{1}{2}
(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})^T
\mathbf{A}
(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP}).
$$

where:

$$
\mathbf{A}
=
\mathbf{M}^T\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{M}
+
\lambda\mathbf{H}.
$$

Substitute into the evidence integral:

$$
P(\mathbf{d}\mid \lambda,\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g})
=
\frac{|\mathbf{C}^{-1}|^{1/2}}
{(2\pi)^{N_d/2}}
\frac{|\lambda\mathbf{H}|^{1/2}}
{(2\pi)^{N_s/2}}
\exp[-E(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})]
\int
\exp
\left[
-\frac{1}{2}
\Delta\mathbf{s}^T
\mathbf{A}
\Delta\mathbf{s}
\right]
d\mathbf{s}.
$$

The Gaussian integral is:

$$
\int
\exp
\left[
-\frac{1}{2}
\Delta\mathbf{s}^T
\mathbf{A}
\Delta\mathbf{s}
\right]
d\mathbf{s}
=
(2\pi)^{N_s/2}
|\mathbf{A}|^{-1/2}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(\mathbf{d}\mid \lambda,\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g})
=
\frac{|\mathbf{C}^{-1}|^{1/2}}
{(2\pi)^{N_d/2}}
|\lambda\mathbf{H}|^{1/2}
|\mathbf{A}|^{-1/2}
\exp[-E_D(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})-\lambda E_R(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})].
$$

Taking the log:

$$
\log P
=
-E_D(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})
-\lambda E_R(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})
-\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{A}|
+
\frac{1}{2}\log|\lambda\mathbf{H}|
+
\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{C}^{-1}|
-
\frac{N_d}{2}\log(2\pi).
$$

Using:

$$
|\lambda\mathbf{H}|
=
\lambda^{N_s}|\mathbf{H}|,
$$

we get:

$$
\frac{1}{2}\log|\lambda\mathbf{H}|
=
\frac{N_s}{2}\log\lambda
+
\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{H}|.
$$

Thus:

$$
\log P
=
-E_D(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})
-\lambda E_R(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})
-\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{A}|
+
\frac{N_s}{2}\log\lambda
+
\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{H}|
+
\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{C}^{-1}|
-
\frac{N_d}{2}\log(2\pi).
$$

---

## 10. Meaning of the Evidence Terms

The log evidence contains:

$$
\log P
=
-E_D(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})
-\lambda E_R(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})
-\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{A}|
+
\frac{N_s}{2}\log\lambda
+
\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{H}|
+
\text{constant}.
$$

### 10.1 Data Fit Term

$$
-E_D(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})
$$

This rewards source reconstructions that fit the observed image well.

### 10.2 Regularization Penalty Term

$$
-\lambda E_R(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})
$$

This penalizes sources that are too rough, too large, or too complex according to the chosen regularization.

### 10.3 Occam Term

$$
-\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{A}|
$$

This comes from integrating over the posterior volume around the most probable source.

The Gaussian integral produces:

$$
|\mathbf{A}|^{-1/2}.
$$

Taking the log gives:

$$
-\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{A}|.
$$

Here, $$\mathbf{A}$$ is the posterior precision matrix. If $$\mathbf{A}$$ has large eigenvalues, the posterior is narrow, meaning only a small volume of source space fits the data and prior.

More precisely, the Occam factor is the determinant ratio:

$$
\frac{1}{2}
\log
\frac{|\lambda\mathbf{H}|}{|\mathbf{A}|}.
$$

This compares the posterior volume to the prior volume.

### 10.4 Prior Normalization Term

$$
\frac{N_s}{2}\log\lambda
$$

This comes from the normalization of the Gaussian source prior.

Because:

$$
|\lambda\mathbf{H}|
=
\lambda^{N_s}|\mathbf{H}|,
$$

the prior normalization contains:

$$
\frac{N_s}{2}\log\lambda.
$$

Increasing $$\lambda$$ makes the prior narrower and taller.

### 10.5 Regularization Determinant Term

$$
\frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{H}|
$$

This also comes from the normalization of the Gaussian source prior.

It depends on the regularization form. Zeroth-order, gradient, and curvature regularization have different $$\mathbf{H}$$ matrices and therefore different determinant terms.

For a fixed regularization type, this term is constant with respect to $$\lambda$$. It matters when comparing different regularization forms.

---

## 11. Tuning the Regularization Strength

For each regularization type, the optimal regularization strength is chosen by maximizing the evidence:

$$
\hat{\lambda}
=
\arg\max_{\lambda}
\log P(\mathbf{d}\mid \lambda,\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}).
$$

In practice:

1. choose a regularization form;
2. choose a trial $$\lambda$$;
3. solve the linear source inversion;
4. compute the log evidence;
5. vary $$\lambda$$;
6. choose the $$\lambda$$ that maximizes the evidence.

The paper also expresses the optimum condition using:

$$
2\lambda E_R(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})
=
\gamma,
$$

where:

$$
\gamma
=
N_s
-
\lambda
\mathrm{Tr}
\left(
\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{H}
\right).
$$

Here $$\gamma$$ is the effective number of source degrees of freedom constrained by the data.

---

## 12. Evidence Interpretation

The evidence balances data fit and source simplicity.

Small $$\lambda$$:

- weak regularization;
- source is flexible;
- image fit can improve;
- source may fit noise;
- evidence penalizes unused flexibility.

Large $$\lambda$$:

- strong regularization;
- source becomes smooth/simple;
- image fit worsens;
- evidence decreases if the source becomes too restrictive.

Optimal $$\lambda$$:

- best compromise between image fit and source complexity.

Compact form:

$$
\log P
=
\underbrace{-E_D(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})}_{\text{data fit}}
+
\underbrace{-\lambda E_R(\mathbf{s}_{\rm MP})}_{\text{regularization penalty}}
+
\underbrace{
\frac{1}{2}
\log
\frac{|\lambda\mathbf{H}|}{|\mathbf{A}|}
}_{\text{Occam factor}}
+
\text{constants}.
$$

---

## 13. Results for the Two-Gaussian Source Example

For the simulated two-Gaussian source example, the paper reports evidence values for the three regularization types.

| Regularization | Optimal $$\hat{\lambda}$$ | Log evidence |
|---|---:|---:|
| Zeroth-order | 17.7 | 5086 |
| Gradient | 34.2 | 5367 |
| Curvature | 68.5 | 5410 |

The highest evidence is obtained by curvature regularization.

Thus, for the smooth Gaussian source example, the Bayesian evidence prefers curvature regularization.

## 15. Key Takeaway

The paper provides a classical Bayesian framework for pixelated source reconstruction in strong lensing.

For a fixed lens model:

$$
\mathbf{d}
=
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{s}
+
\mathbf{n}
$$

is linear in the source, so the regularized source reconstruction can be solved by a linear system.

The source prior is imposed through quadratic regularization:

$$
E_R(\mathbf{s})
=
\frac{1}{2}
\mathbf{s}^T\mathbf{H}\mathbf{s}.
$$

The regularization strength $$\lambda$$ is chosen by maximizing the Bayesian evidence:

$$
P(\mathbf{d}\mid \lambda,\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g})
=
\int
P(\mathbf{d}\mid \mathbf{s},\mathbf{f})
P(\mathbf{s}\mid \lambda,\mathbf{g})
\,d\mathbf{s}.
$$

The most important conceptual point is that Bayesian evidence does not only reward a good image fit. It also penalizes unnecessary source flexibility through the Occam factor.

This makes it a principled method for selecting regularization strength and comparing regularization forms.
